I've been suffering from an unquenchable thirst for the answer to one question - "Who am I?" - for most of my life. And now that I have a reasonable argument to make, I will try my best to answer this question to my own satisfaction.

### So Artemis, what should a thought machine think about itself?

This feels like a trivial question to solve, right? If I am nothing but a thought machine, why think of myself as something more or less? Why define more convoluted philosophical arguments for a solved issue?

The problem with the thought machine argument is that, for better or for worse, it only describes the macroscopic behaviour of humans, as a member of a society and while surrounded by other members of the same society. Our alien observer made vague observations about an abstract human, and not a person. In fact, when you extend the thought machine argument to an individual - it breaks down. Individuals do lots of things *just for the sake of it*.

A bathroom singer never shares his terrible singing voice with anyone, it isn't impressive in the slightest, and doesn't seem to be getting any better at it, bath after bath! So then why, why does a *thought machine* indulge in *mindless pleasures*? Biologically, the answer is simple, an incomprehensible mashup of biochemical reactions in your skull makes you do anything at all. But to understand human actions beyond this definition, one must understand individualism philosophically.

### What makes individuals unique?

For the sake of this definition, we will think from the top down. Imagine you're an alien, trying to find one particular person in the sea of billions. How will you approach this problem?

Well, there's all the external stuff. Some people live in large, segregated houses. Some have fancy identities, fame, power and wealth that marks them. But what about the rest of us? How do you differentiate between two people living in seemingly identical concrete blocks, with seemingly identical lives? *What remains, once you strip people bare of their external lives?*

While we used the word "experiences" to sweep this question under the rug in 002, how exactly do these experiences differentiate people? The naive answer is to think "Oh, it's how they think! Isn't that the thought machine argument again?" And to discuss why that isn't sufficient, we must explore the uniqueness of thought.

### On sequentiality and the uniqueness of thought.

Observationally, we can see that human thoughts are sequential - They follow one after the other, being a direct result of a previous thought or an external stimuli (hence the term "train" of thoughts). What's worse, Humans don't think just one thing at once, given how neurons function. Sure, one thought will *dominate*, but hundreds if not thousands of possibilities will be processed in the human mind along with it.

Hence, once you start examining human thoughts for signs of uniqueness, you run into the issue that - Yes, while each individual has its neurons uniquely arranged due to a combination of biology and lived experiences, you're providing everyone with the same external stimulus. Hence, the train (really, it's more like a bunch of "clouds" linked together) of thought to follow, you will find different permutations of the same thought.

It's a highly specialised system converging on the same output, even with millions of different pathways! That's efficient, and it ensures survival. But, for our purposes, *It is not unique.*

### So then, what's a better definition?

One question we must answer before we can answer individuality is "Does anyone have anything truly unique?" It can't be *just* experiences, after all, people around the world who have gone through the same childhood turn out to be really different people. And it can't be *just* the arrangement of their neurons, since there's only so many ways you can arrange them. One might feel like the answer to this question is "no", or the unscientific "conciousness", or even the unhelpful "DNA", but we can do better.

A simple solution would be to fight the odds by combining arguments - it's pretty much impossible for people with the same social experiences to have the same neural structure, given how the brain develops. Since neural pathways build in a probabilistic process, and grow differently even in the same environment - this is the closest we can get to uniqueness.

Hence, our better definition of a person would combine what a person thinks, and how they express their experiences. Hence, **we define individuality as the material result of how thoughts are conveyed**. Individuality is how a thought machine *shares* its thoughts - not just in the broad sense, in the physical sense.

For example, draw a cat. No actually, do it. Pick up a pen and paper or your tablet and doodle a cat.

Do you think anyone, out of the billions in the world, drew the same cat as you? And would write the same poem on the cat as you? And would you sing to the cat in the same way you do? *Obviously not, they don't have the experience in drawing/ writing/ singing that you do. Nor will they have thought of the same cat.*

### So, Artemis, what is a person?

In the end, a human is a thought machine, and a person is the thought machine's channel of conveying its thoughts. Any action done by a person is either conveying its thoughts to itself, other people, or creating experiences out of the thoughts. *When humans get better at expressing themselves, they literally become better people.*

The act of expressing yourself, being creative, making things for the sake of making them is the greatest honor you can bestow upon yourself. Creating for yourself doesn't just make you better at expressing your thoughts, it makes you have better thoughts. Creativity isn't just a person's trait, it's *the person itself*.

Never stop creating.
